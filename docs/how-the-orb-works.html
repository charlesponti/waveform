An orb that listens

Every dot is one moment of sound at one pitch. Hum, whistle or drum and different parts of the sphere move. This page builds it from the version that does not work to the one that does, with the maths and the engineering at each step.

Synthetic sounds, drawn the way the app draws a live microphone. Pick a different one in the bar above, and press Sound to hear it.

Start here / the obvious version

One number, one sphere

A sphere of dots. Measure how loud the sound is right now. Push every dot out by that amount. It takes about ten lines and it does react to sound.

// every audio buffer
let loudness = buffer.map(abs).max()   // 0...1

// every screen frame
for dot in dots {
  dot.radius = 1 + 0.5 * loudness
}

Try the Arpeggio, then the Hiss, then the Vowels. They are very different sounds and the orb does the same thing for all of them: it breathes. It cannot say which pitch, it has no memory of what came before, and the amber line on the right moves in steps.

Part 2 / maths and engineering

Cut time into exact columns

Everything after this works on columns: fixed slices of time, each summarised by the lowest sample, the highest sample and the root mean square. The orb never touches raw samples again.

The edges have to be exact. A phone records at 44.1 or 48 kHz, and 120 columns a second does not divide 44,100. A column is 367.5 samples long. Truncate it to 367 and every column is a little short, so after a minute the picture has run ahead of the sound.

// the edge of column k is placed from the running sample count
static func edge(_ k: Int, _ sampleRate: Double, _ rate: Double) -> Int {
  Int((Double(k) * sampleRate / rate).rounded())
}
edge(k) = round( k · sampleRate / columnsPerSecond ) no running sum of rounded lengths, so errors cannot add up

Buffers from the microphone do not line up with columns either. The builder takes whatever it is given (512 samples, 441, 1) and finishes a column wherever an edge falls inside the buffer. The three summaries come from single vDSP calls, so this is cheap enough for the audio thread.

Part 3 / maths

Loudness without flicker

Root mean square is the sound's energy rather than its biggest swing:

rms = √( (x₁² + x₂² + … + xₙ²) / n )

A voice at 120 Hz repeats every 8.3 ms. If a column is shorter than that, its rms depends on which part of the wave it landed on: near a crest it is high, near a zero crossing it is low. Neighbouring columns disagree and the orb shimmers, even though the voice is steady.

The fix is to measure over a window of a few pitch periods. The builder keeps the last 25 ms of column sums and reports the rms of all of them. Drag the column length down and watch the amber line start to shake while the mint one holds still.

Then the shape of the response. Instant attack, so a hit shows at once. A release with a time constant (150 ms by default), so a sound fades the way a meter needle does, not as a flash.

level[k] = max( rms[k], level[k−1] · e^(−1 / (rate · release)) )

Last, the scale. Speech spans 60 dB or more, so linear height makes a quiet word invisible next to a loud one. Heights are decibels:

dB = 20 · log₁₀(amplitude) height = clamp( (dB − floor) / −floor, 0, 1 ) floor = −60 dB

Part 4 / maths

Time on a sphere

Dots need to sit evenly on a sphere, with no clumps at the poles and no visible rows. The Fibonacci lattice does that with one loop. Heights are evenly spaced, and each dot is turned by the golden angle from the one before.

yᵢ = 1 − 2(i + ½) / N θᵢ = i · π(3 − √5) ≈ 137.5°, the golden angle (x, z) = √(1 − y²) · (cos θ, sin θ)

Height gives age. The equator is the newest column and the poles are the oldest, so each new sound appears as a ring at the equator and spreads toward both poles as it ages:

age = |asin y| / (π/2) 0 at the equator, 1 at a pole column shown = playhead − age · history

The history slider is how many seconds the sphere spans. The orb in the app uses 0.8.

Part 5 / maths and engineering

A playhead on the audio clock

A screen frame should not ask "what is the newest column?". Columns appear when the audio thread is scheduled, which jitters, so the newest column is a moving, stepping target. Instead the audio thread tells a shared clock when each buffer started, and the screen asks "where in the stream is now, minus a fixed delay?"

// StreamClock: smooth the offset between audio time and host time
smoothed += gain * (raw - smoothed)       // gain = 0.02

// Playhead: draw a fixed moment behind the audio
position = min(newest - 1,
               streamTime(hostNow - latency) * rate - 0.5)

The latency (60 ms) covers the audio buffer, the time to finish a column, and the one column of look-ahead the interpolation needs.

The system also reports frame times with a millisecond or two of noise. Used raw, that noise becomes shake. A phase-locked loop follows the steady beat of the frames:

predicted = phase + period
error = clamp(measured - predicted, -period, 2 * period)
period += 0.01 * error
phase  = predicted + 0.15 * error

Last, the playhead lands between two columns. Reading the nearest one gives steps. Linear gives a corner at every column. Catmull-Rom runs a cubic through the four neighbours, so the value and its slope are both continuous:

p(t) = ½ · [ 2p₁ + (p₂ − p₀)t + (2p₀ − 5p₁ + 4p₂ − p₃)t² + (3p₁ − p₀ − 3p₂ + p₃)t³ ]

Part 6 / maths

Pitch: a spectrum every column

For each column the last N samples go through a Hann window and a Fourier transform. A window is needed because the transform assumes the slice repeats forever. Cut a tone off sharply and the seam spreads its energy across every frequency. Switch the window off on the right to see the skirts.

w[n] = ½ − ½ cos(2πn / N)

The numbers are scaled so they mean something. A pure tone of amplitude A should read A/√2, its rms, in whichever band it falls in. With Σw² the window's energy and X the transform:

power[k] = 2 · |X[k]|² / (N · Σw²) band power = Σ power[k] over the bins inside the band band value = √(band power) a sine A gives A / √2

Bands are 24 steps on a log scale from 80 Hz to 8 kHz, so each is the same musical width (a little over three semitones). A band edge rarely falls on a bin, so the bin it cuts through is counted by the fraction inside. The band powers add up to the signal's mean square, which the tests check.

Speech loses 6 dB or more per octave, so the top bands would sit near silence. A display tilt adds tilt dB per octave above 1 kHz. Set it to 0 and the numbers are exact.

One more detail: a window of N samples describes the moment half a window ago. The orb reads the spectrum N/2 samples later than the loudness, so a vowel's pitch and its volume line up.

A sine of amplitude 0.5 should read 0.354 and have mean square 0.125.
Part 7 / engineering

Two threads, no lock

The microphone callback runs on a real-time thread with a deadline of a few milliseconds. It must never wait for the screen, take a lock, allocate memory, or print. The screen thread has to read what it wrote.

The answer is a ring: a fixed array and one counter. The audio thread writes into the slot, then publishes the new count with a release store. The screen thread loads the count with an acquire load, so it can never see a slot that is still being filled.

func append(_ column: Column) {            // audio thread only
  let index = written.load(ordering: .relaxed)
  slots[index & mask] = column
  written.store(index + 1, ordering: .releasing)
}

func snapshot(...) {                       // screen thread only
  while true {
    let end = written.load(ordering: .acquiring)
    copy the newest n slots
    // the writer lapped us mid-copy? throw it away and copy again
    if written.load(ordering: .acquiring) - (end - real) <= capacity { return }
  }
}

The size is a power of two, so wrapping is a bitwise and instead of a modulo. The retry means what is drawn is never a mixture of old and new. Press the button to force the writer to lap the reader and watch the copy start again.

Part 8 / engineering

Proving the maths

The risk in a picture is that it looks plausible while being wrong. So each piece of maths has a test with a known answer, written from the definition and not from what the code happens to output.

The tests run on the Mac with synthetic signals and need no microphone. There are 33 across the package.

PropertyHow it is checked
Exact columnsFeed audio in buffers of many sizes; the column count and edges match the sample count every time.
A / √2A pure sine of amplitude A reads A/√2 in its spectrum band.
EnergyThe squares of the band values add up to the signal's mean square.
Aligned hopsSpectrum frame k and column k end on the same sample.
No flickerA windowed loudness varies far less than a single column's on a steady tone.
Pitch mapEach dot's pitch equals its longitude from the definition, the same on both sides.
Torn readsA reader that is lapped by the writer retries and returns whole columns.
Part 9 / engineering

Does it need Metal?

The orb is 1,800 dots. The work per frame is small: about eight table reads per dot, then a rotation and a circle. Two choices keep it cheap in SwiftUI's Canvas. Dots are sorted into four depth layers and each layer is a single path with a single fill, so there are four draw calls and not 1,800. And nothing allocates inside the frame.

A readout in the corner of the app shows the average draw time. The rule for going to Metal is written down before measuring:

draw time > ~4 ms at 120 Hz, or dropped frames → Metal (a 120 Hz frame is 8.3 ms; leave half of it for the system)

What Metal would add: exact frame pacing with CAMetalDisplayLink, twenty to fifty times more dots, and glow. If the Canvas stays under budget, none of that is worth the extra code.
